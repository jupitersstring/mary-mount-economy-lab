Money, work, ownership and the price of claims

Working thesis. The institutions of money, credit, taxation and ownership influence who first commands resources, who bears risk and who receives productivity gains. That is a strong research programme. It does not establish one universal intention behind two centuries of monetary history, or make hard money sufficient for leisure. This laboratory turns those propositions into explicit, competing mechanisms.

The theory has been tightened before attaching data. Accounting identities are enforced; behavioural rules are exposed; historical interpretations are sourced. A balanced simulation establishes internal consistency. It does not establish that its elasticities, political bundles or projected security values describe a country.

2 · Cantillon, wheat and the difference between dollars and purchasing power

Cantillon examines how additional money enters particular hands, changes spending and relative prices, and passes unevenly through the economy. His treatment of population and employment also gives importance to landowners’ spending choices. It is a theory of transmission and distribution, rather than proof that every monetary authority intends permanent toil. Relevant passages are Part I, chapters XIV–XVI, and Part II, chapters VI–VIII. Cantillon, Essay on the Nature of Trade in General, Parts I–III.

The wheat puzzle needs three measurements: the dollar price per comparable unit, the price relative to a consumption basket, and the hours of work needed to buy it. A gain in harvesting labour productivity does not equal the same gain in total wheat production productivity. Fertiliser, machinery, energy, land, storage, transport, quality, trade and markups can change separately. The claimed historical 5–20× range has not been verified for a matched task, country and interval.

Illustration: real unit cost = labour-cost share / labour-productivity multiplier + other-cost share × other-input cost multiplier.

With a 30% labour share, tenfold harvesting efficiency and unchanged other costs, real unit cost falls to 0.73×. If the common nominal wage and price scale rises tenfold, the dollar cost becomes 7.3× the original while its assumed work-time price falls 27%. This arithmetic explains how nominal inflation and real productivity gains can coexist. It does not identify what caused the nominal change, or show who captured the actual historical gain.

Likewise, MV = PY implies a lower aggregate price level when nominal spending MV is fixed and real output Y rises. Keeping the monetary base fixed does not by itself keep broad credit money or velocity fixed. An identity becomes a causal model only after these behaviours and institutions have been specified.

3 · Work, abundance and the poverty claim

Keynes’s 1930 essay imagines substantial productivity growth eventually easing absolute material needs and opening room for leisure, while distinguishing relative wants. It makes conditions concerning war and population. It does not demonstrate that a hard monetary standard is necessary or sufficient for the outcome. Mill’s stationary-state discussion offers a separate classical case for improvements in distribution, culture and life without making expansion the sole objective. Keynes, Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren (1930); Mill, Principles of Political Economy, Book IV, chapter VI.

A higher real wage can buy the same basket with fewer hours: the income effect. It also raises the opportunity cost of an hour of leisure: the substitution effect. If housing, debt service, aspirations or insecurity rise, the first channel may be overwhelmed. Someone without access to adequate assets or a productive occupation cannot necessarily choose a lower-work life. Balboni and co-authors find evidence of an asset threshold and occupational constraints among poor households in Bangladesh; that is direct evidence against treating all poverty as a taste for leisure, although it is not a universal model of every country. Balboni et al., Why Do People Stay Poor? (2021 working paper).

The lab therefore exposes both wage elasticities and an essential-budget constraint. Hours, employment utilisation and purchasing power are separate. Broader ownership can supply dividends, rent or bank income; an opening ownership allocation is a different counterfactual economy, not a costless mid-run redistribution. Wage households and asset households are not age cohorts. Pensions can go to either group, and their allocation is visible.

Fisher’s debt-deflation analysis supplies a crucial counterweight: falling prices can raise the real burden of fixed nominal commitments and intensify distress when borrowers try to liquidate together. Productivity-led price declines and a debt-driven collapse should not be assumed to have the same welfare effects. The work view therefore shows real household debt as well as interest service; defaults and forced asset sales remain outside this small model. Fisher, The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions (1933).

4 · Why these institutions emerged, and who argued for them

There was no single unchanged monetary constitution over the last 200 years. A defensible answer distinguishes institutional motives from later theories and critiques. War funding, commercial settlement, capital mobilisation, crisis response and macroeconomic stabilisation are overlapping rationales.

Wicksell’s mechanism is not a universal statement that lower rates always raise output or shares. Creditworthy demand, investment opportunities, repayment risk, prices and fiscal responses can offset it. The present interest-gap rule is an original modelling choice, not a reconstruction of his complete theory.

5 · Fisher and Benes–Kumhof: plans, not a hard-money synonym

Fisher’s 100% Money

Fisher proposed full backing for checking deposits and a separation between money creation and lending. His objective included preventing both inflation and deflation; it was not simply a permanently fixed money quantity. Credit financed by savings or risk-bearing capital remains conceptually distinct from transaction money. See chapters III–VI of Fisher, 100% Money (1935; revised 1936), primary book. The edition and contents were checked; no claim is made that this app reproduces every statutory detail.

The Chicago Plan Revisited

Benes and Kumhof formalise a reform in a DSGE model: reserve backing, Treasury credit, public-debt offsets and private-debt reductions have linked balance-sheet effects. Their treatment distinguishes government-issued money from conventional interest-bearing debt and describes irredeemable money as commonwealth equity. Their simulated benefits depend on their model and transition assumptions. The working paper states that it does not represent IMF policy. See introduction, section IV and transition section VI in Benes and Kumhof, The Chicago Plan Revisited, WP/12/202 (2012).

What is actually implemented

The Money plans view supplies two original transition illustrations. In the Fisher illustration, the public issuer acquires investment-fund units when it supplies reserve money. Payment deposits are backed; risky fund units finance lending. In the Kumhof illustration, reserves arrive with Treasury credit, bank-held public bills can be offset, and private relief reduces public net worth by the exact benefit granted to debtors. Every financial asset has a liability counterpart.

The dynamic core currently uses accommodating Treasury credit against reserve backing. That makes its bank accounts explicit, but does not decouple the money and credit quantities as a complete reform regime would. It is a comparison of backing and public funding, not an exact Fisher lending market or a replication of the Benes–Kumhof DSGE. Neither the authors’ output gain nor a prediction of eliminated crises has been imported.

Judge each plan by separate questions: Who chooses money growth? How is new credit funded? Who bears credit losses? What happens to bank capital and existing contracts? What fiscal assets or liabilities change? Which nonbank claims can still run? How are transition gains distributed? Full backing alone answers only part of that institutional design.

6 · Tax, public debt, demographics and international power

Private bank lending and public deficits must not be conflated. A loan produces a borrower liability as well as a deposit. A deficit changes public liabilities and other sectors’ positions. Taxation changes spending power and distribution; its effect depends on who pays, on real capacity and on the monetary arrangement. Spending can compete for scarce labour and materials even when its nominal funding is available.

The ratio of public debt to GDP depends on growth, effective interest, primary balances and other stock changes. A simplified approximation is Δb ≈ (r − g)b − primary surplus/GDP, with consistent nominal or real definitions. Blanchard examines circumstances under which low interest relative to growth can make rollover feasible, while distinguishing fiscal cost from welfare and risk. This is neither proof that all debt burdens unborn taxpayers nor a guarantee of free finance. Blanchard, Public Debt and Low Interest Rates (2019).

Ageing may increase pensions per worker, but participation, productivity, pension rules and ownership of claims matter. Government debt held domestically is an asset to someone; external debt has a foreign claimant. A complete intergenerational welfare model needs overlapping age cohorts, mortality, bequests, household heterogeneity and the value of public assets. This app provides demographic pressure indicators, not that full generational accounting.

Historical experience includes non-hegemonic countries overcoming foreign-currency borrowing constraints; local-currency borrowing is not exclusive to the United States. That does not mean every country can copy any deficit path safely. The lab separates a domestic sovereign spread from translation of unhedged foreign-currency debt. It does not estimate reserve-currency demand, capital flight or exchange-rate collapse. Bordo, Meissner and Redish, How Original Sin Was Overcome (2003).

7 · Reconcile policy with financial markets

Policy → demand, wages, prices, financing and investment → sales and operating margins → tax and reinvestment → distributable cash → required returns and terminal conditions → value.

This chain is conditional, not an automatic positive relation between money growth and share prices. More sales can coexist with lower margins. More profit can coexist with less free cash flow if reinvestment rises. Higher earnings can coexist with a lower multiple if required returns rise. A listed company’s foreign sales and ownership also prevent the domestic economy and its listed equity index from being identical populations.

GDP is value added, whereas sales include intermediate transactions. The lab adds an explicit corporate turnover/value-added assumption and separates land rent from corporate surplus. It labels market capitalisation/GDP accurately. P/E uses net common earnings; P/S uses company or explicitly constructed corporate sales. EV uses financing claims matched to its operating denominator. Damodaran, valuation teaching materials; Damodaran, relative valuation and firm-value multiples.

P/S = P/E × net profit margin.
EV/Sales = EV/EBITDA × EBITDA margin.
FCFE = net income + depreciation − investment + net borrowing = corporate distributions in this closure.
Common equity = operating value − debt − preferred − NCI − debt-like claims + excess cash + separable non-operating assets.

The aggregate equity series discounts announced future distributions. The issuer satellite discounts operating cash flows, incorporates working capital, then applies its claims bridge. Its macro demand sensitivity, pricing pass-through and labour efficiency are editable assumptions. It is a satellite, not a second copy of the corporate sector added to national accounts. Terminal growth requires reinvestment through g/ROIC or g/ROE; invalid terminal spreads are shown as unavailable. Damodaran, uValue book, growth and reinvestment.

Unknown claims do not become zeros. A different quote currency requires explicit conversion to the reporting currency. Non-positive denominators withhold earnings multiples. A nonzero capitalised lease claim is withheld against this cash-rent forecast until a matching lease model exists. The reference equity quote divided by future scenario earnings is labelled as such; no future market quote is being predicted.

8 · Political archetypes without false precision

The eleven starting interpretations are reference mixed credit, Smith, Wicksell, Keynes, Mill, George, Schumpeter, broader ownership, Fisher, Benes–Kumhof and a monetary-rule family. Their numerical values are lab design choices, not measured party positions or tax rates proposed by those authors. George’s land-rent capture is anchored in his own proposal, but the lab has no endogenous land-use response. George, Progress and Poverty, Book VIII, chapter II.

Six meta controls change fiscal intensity, ownership breadth, sufficiency preferences, public reserve backing, research investment and rent capture. Each applies a visible mapping to individual assumptions. Slider endpoints are moderate demonstration ranges qualified at the reference settings, not ideological maxima or a complete feasible frontier. A strong public-money policy can coexist with private credit allocation; a smaller state can coexist with strict banking rules. Political systems cannot be represented faithfully by a single left/right slider. No regime receives a composite “best” score.

9 · Experiments worth using

Compare seventeen computed experiments and their conditional lessons. Each uses a matched reference and identifies its opening conditions.

  1. Incidence: keep transfers fixed and change recipients. Then equalise spending propensities. The aggregate effect should disappear when only irrelevant labels change.
  2. Abundance: raise productivity under stronger income effects, then stronger substitution effects. Compare hours, wage purchasing power and essential shortfalls.
  3. Debt pressure: first set household debt above zero; raise its spread. Compare required hours and debt service. With zero borrowing stock, the direct household-interest channel should do nothing.
  4. Land: begin with a positive rent share; compare lower wage tax with higher land-rent tax. Account for the loss to existing owners rather than calling it newly created wealth.
  5. Innovation: compare identical research costs with and without a productivity payoff. A financing boom is not itself evidence of social gain.
  6. War and construction: compare equal purchases with different public-investment shares. The model omits destruction and military security benefits, so this is a resource-allocation experiment only.
  7. Ageing: raise the retired share and pension commitment; then separately raise participation or productivity. Inspect liabilities and pension/wage ratios.
  8. Reform: inspect Fisher and Kumhof ledgers before changing reserve backing. Compare net interest-bearing debt with broad public net liabilities and the debt-relief counterpart.
  9. Markets: hold operating flows fixed while changing required returns; then change turnover without changing GDP. Observe which multiples change and why.

10 · Evidence needed before policy or investment use

The current model is a small original synthesis with stock-flow discipline inspired by Godley and Lavoie, Kaleckian Models of Growth in a Coherent Stock-Flow Monetary Framework. It lacks defaults, insolvency resolution, capital adequacy, a full labour market, sectoral input-output structure, endogenous migration/fertility, overlapping generations, land development, an exchange-rate market and a separate nonbank financial system. Production is a soft capacity benchmark; demand may temporarily exceed it. Public capital supplies capacity services but government output and its wage bill are not a separate production sector.

The included US snapshot supplies context and three same-quarter expenditure anchors, not an estimated economy. The issuer JSON provides a practical route to sourced company inputs. Before treating results as decisions, fit and challenge behavioural parameters, use matched point-in-time data, add necessary institutional closures, compare alternative specifications, and test historical episodes outside the calibration sample. Neither balanced accounts nor attractive plots establish empirical causation or investment alpha.

Answer to the challenge. There are explicit rationales and identifiable proponents behind particular institutions: mobilisation of public and private resources, commerce, settlement, crisis liquidity and stabilisation. There are also coherent critiques of credit instability, ownership concentration and distribution. The fruitful question is which institution, which constraint, which beneficiaries and which measurable counterfactual—not a claim that one unchanged system has had one demonstrable purpose for 200 years.
